The honest answer is that the season matters a great deal for the inspection and hardly at all for the repair.

## Why the Season Changes What You Can See

Birmingham sits on clay with a high shrink-swell potential. It takes on water and expands through the wet months, then gives that water up and contracts through the dry ones. Roughly fifty-four inches of rain a year, arriving in bursts between hot dry spells, means the ground under your house is rarely the same volume in August as it was in March.

That has a direct consequence for diagnosis. In late summer and early fall, when the clay has shrunk as far as it is going to, settlement is at its most pronounced. Doors that stick only in September, a crack that opens a little each August, a gap under a baseboard that closes by Christmas — that is the season showing you the full range of movement.

Inspect the same house in February, after months of rain have swollen the soil back up, and some of those symptoms will have partly closed. Not because anything was fixed, but because the ground pushed back up. It is entirely possible to look at a house in late winter and underestimate what it does in a bad year.

So if you have a choice and the damage is not urgent, an inspection at the end of a dry spell tells you more.

## Why the Season Barely Changes the Repair

Piers are installed to reach load-bearing soil or rock beneath the active zone — the upper few feet where seasonal moisture actually swings. Once the pier is bearing on something stable, it stops caring what the surface clay is doing that month. That is the entire point of the method.

So the repair itself works year round. Crews work through Alabama winters without much difficulty, and the ground rarely freezes hard enough here to be a factor the way it is further north.

There are practical differences rather than structural ones. Excavation in saturated clay is slower, messier and harder on your yard. Interior work like crawl space encapsulation is genuinely season-independent. And most contractors are busiest in spring, after homeowners spend a wet winter noticing new cracks, so scheduling in late fall or winter is often quicker.

## The Argument Against Waiting

Foundation movement is not self-limiting. A crack that has opened by a couple of millimeters this year does not stop because you would prefer to deal with it in October. Meanwhile the gap lets water in, water softens the soil further, and the next dry spell has more room to work with.

The cases that turn expensive are almost always the ones that were left. A house needing four piers is a manageable job. The same house three years later, with a bowed wall and a sagging floor system on top of the original settlement, is a different quote entirely.

## What We Would Actually Suggest

If you have noticed something and it is currently dry, book the inspection now while the symptoms are at their clearest.

If you have noticed something and it is the middle of a wet winter, still book it. A competent inspection accounts for the season, and an assessment that says "this is what I can see now, and here is what I would expect to see in September" is more useful than another nine months of guessing.

If nothing is wrong and you are simply being cautious, late summer is the time to walk the house and look properly, because that is when it will tell you the truth.
